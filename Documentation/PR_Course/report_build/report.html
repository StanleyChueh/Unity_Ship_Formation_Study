Summary. Both members of our group track one target with a camera, using the same pipeline: YOLOv8 finds the target, Lucas-Kanade optical flow moves the box between detections, and a Kalman filter combines them. One platform is a drone; the other is a fleet of unmanned surface vessels (USVs) that keep formation by following a leader boat. Both break down when the target's box moves up and down quickly in the image. On the drone the target climbs and drops; on the USV the camera itself pitches in waves. The current tracker depends on hand-tuned constants. We replace them with course methods: maximum-likelihood estimation (Ch. 3-1) of the filter's noise, a minimum-risk Bayes rule (Ch. 2) for accepting or rejecting each detection, and PCA, Fisher LDA and the Hughes effect (Ch. 3-2, Ch. 1) for choosing features. A first offline test already shows the main effect. Choosing the process noise by maximum likelihood turns the drone tracker from worse than raw YOLO on the boat (73% vs 85% of frames tracked well in clear water) into better than it (88%). On the drone it lifts accuracy from 47–57% to 72–82% when the detector is slow.

Part I. Midterm proposal (Question 1)

1. Topic

Problem. A camera tracker has to decide two things in every frame: which detected box is the target, and how much to trust it compared with its own prediction. In our current code, both decisions use hand-tuned constants: a distance gate (GATE = 1.5), a size-ratio window (0.25 to 5), measurement noise R = 60·(0.5/confidence)², and a fixed process noise Q that was "raised by hand so the filter keeps up when the drone accelerates". These values were tuned for a 30 fps, 1080p drone video. They have no basis in data and do not carry over to another platform.

Shared difficulty. Fast vertical motion of the target's box. On our USV logs, the share of frames where YOLO misses the leader grows from 1.8% to 14.7% as the box's vertical speed rises from 0–2 to 20–40 px per step. The share where YOLO labels the wrong box as the leader grows from 0.5% to 7.6% (Figure 1).

Idea. Treat each hand-tuned decision as a pattern recognition problem and learn it from labelled data:

Figure 0. The shared tracker. Blue boxes are where the course methods replace hand-tuned constants.

The filter state is x = (cx, cy, w, h, vx, vy, vw, vh)t, with a constant-velocity transition F. A measurement z = Hx + v (a YOLO box or a flow-shifted box) has noise v ~ N(0, R). The motion model is xk+1 = Fxk + wk, with wk ~ N(0, Q). Errors are measured in units of box size, so one model works for both near and far targets.

II.2 Method 1: maximum-likelihood estimate of the measurement noise R (Ch. 3-1)

For each training detection that matches the true box, the error is ek = (zk − zktrue)/size. With e ~ N(μ, Σ), the slides' Gaussian case with unknown μ and Σ gives the closed-form MLE:

μ̂ = (1/n) Σk ek,    Σ̂ = (1/n) Σk (ek − μ̂)(ek − μ̂)t

We fit Σ̂ separately for the horizontal and vertical axes and for each YOLO-confidence bin. This tests the drone code's assumption that a single variance, scaled by confidence, holds on both axes.

II.3 Method 2: maximum-likelihood estimate of the process noise Q (Ch. 3-1)

(a) With ground truth: closed form, as in the textbook. From the true box trajectory, the process residuals are wk = xk+1 − Fxk, and the MLE is Q̂ = (1/n) Σk wkwkt. This is the same derivation as for R, applied to the motion instead of the measurement.

(b) Without ground truth: innovation likelihood (Mehra, 1970). For a correct model, the innovations νk = zk − Hx̂k|k−1 are independent, with νk ~ N(0, Sk), where Sk = HPk|k−1Ht + R. So we choose Q to maximise

ℓ(Q) = Σk ln N(νk; 0, Sk(Q)) = −½ Σk [ νktSk−1νk + ln|Sk| + d ln 2π ]

This is the Ch. 3-1 principle: the observed samples are the innovations, and Q is the parameter. Because Sk depends on Q through the filter's recursion, there is no closed-form solution, so we maximise over a grid. Method (b) is what runs on a real drone or boat. Method (a) is the textbook check when ground truth exists. The final presentation will compare the two.

II.4 Method 3: minimum-risk Bayes rule for accepting a detection (Ch. 2)

Each candidate box gives a feature vector x: YOLO confidence, Mahalanobis distance to the predicted box under the filter's covariance, log area ratio and log aspect ratio. Classes: ω1 = target, ω2 = not the target. λij is the loss of deciding ωi when the truth is ωj. Accepting a false box (λ12) costs more than rejecting a true one (λ21), because the filter can coast on its prediction for a few frames. Minimising the conditional risk R(αi|x) = Σj λijP(ωj|x) gives the rule: accept if

p(x|ω1) / p(x|ω2) > [(λ12 − λ22) / (λ21 − λ11)] · P(ω2) / P(ω1)

The class-conditional densities p(x|ωi) are Gaussians fitted by MLE, as in Method 1. The priors P(ωi) are estimated per platform and per condition. Distance is measured with the filter's own covariance, so the acceptance region widens automatically while the tracker coasts. This replaces the hand-tuned gate growth of (1 + 0.15·misses).

II.5 Method 4: feature analysis with PCA, Fisher LDA and the Hughes effect (Ch. 3-2, Ch. 1)

Project the candidate features of the three classes (target, other object, clutter) onto the 2-D Fisher subspace (c − 1 = 2 dimensions) for each condition, and show PCA's explained variance. Then measure Bayes-gate accuracy against the number of features d (1 to 9) for training sizes n = 20, 100, 1,000 and 10,000. This reproduces the Hughes peaking effect on our own data and justifies the final feature set.

II.6 Evaluation protocol

  • Deterministic replay. His real-time tracker is replayed frame by frame. A YOLO result requested at frame i arrives at frame i + L. L = 1 matches his GPU (YOLO takes 11 ms; a frame lasts 33 ms); L = 3 imitates a slower onboard computer. On the USV, L is one 100 ms control step. The replay reproduces his reported YOLO detection rate of 83% on testv4.
  • Ground truth. USV: the simulator's true leader box. Drone: Grounding DINO (2 prompts × horizontal flip = 4 opinions, fused with his own labelling rule, without YOLO as an opinion so the labels are independent of the model under test), checked by eye on contact sheets.
  • Training and test data are separate. Drone: trained on one video, tested on the other. USV: runs split by random seed into two folds.
  • Measures. Share of frames with IoU ≥ 0.5 against the true box; the same on the 20% of frames with the fastest vertical motion; wrong-target frames; jitter.

Part III. Preliminary results

III.1 Fast vertical motion breaks detection on the USV

Figure 1. USV, weather-trained YOLO, 259,000 frames. Both error types rise steeply once the box moves more than 10 px per step. In huge waves the median vertical speed doubles (6 vs 3 px per step).

On the drone videos there is no clear trend: misses stay between 7% and 13% at every speed. Drone misses cluster on hard backgrounds instead, such as plants and the white tiled wall in IMG_7888. The per-frame speed is also noisy because the pseudo ground truth jitters. Smoothing and normalising by box size is planned (T2).

III.2 MLE of R: the drone code's noise model is wrong on the vertical axis

Figure 2. USV detection error variance (MLE) by YOLO confidence. Horizontal variance falls about 20× as confidence rises. Vertical variance barely changes, and the vertical error has a constant offset of about +0.09 box heights.

The drone code uses one variance for both axes, scaled by (0.5/confidence)². That roughly fits the horizontal axis but not the vertical one. On the USV, maximum likelihood also shows that optical flow's vertical error variance is about 4× YOLO's (0.045 vs 0.011 box sizes²). So on the boat, flow should carry less weight than YOLO.

III.3 MLE of Q: the likelihood peaks at 10× the drone setting on the USV

Figure 3. USV training fold 0: mean innovation log-likelihood for each candidate Q. Both folds and both R choices peak at 10×. Learned R also gives a higher likelihood than the hand-tuned R (−11.2 vs −12.3), so it describes the data better.

The peak is what the frame rates predict. The drone runs at 30 fps and the boat camera at 10 Hz, so the box moves about 3× as far per step on the boat, and the variance of that motion is about 9× larger. On the drone, the likelihood was still rising at the largest Q tried (100×), so the grid must be widened.

III.7 What the results show

  1. Hand-tuned constants do not transfer. The drone tracker, tuned on drone video, is worse than raw YOLO on the USV in every condition.
  2. MLE-learned Q is the one change that helps on both platforms. On the USV it beats the hand-tuned tracker on all 64 sequences, beats raw YOLO in clear, rain, night and night + rain, ties with YOLO in huge waves, and loses to it in the dark storm. In the fastest huge-wave frames it more than doubles the share of well-tracked frames in the fastest huge-wave frames (9.5% → 22.6%). On the drone with a slow detector it raises accuracy from 47–57% to 72–82%.
  3. Learned R helps only in some cases. It helps on the drone with a 1-frame delay, but hurts with a 3-frame delay and on the USV. The fit compares each box with ground truth at the frame the image was taken, but the filter uses the box L frames later. A latency-aware fit is planned.
  4. The Bayes gate depends on the platform. On the drone, which has real distractors, it gives the best accuracy in fast frames (96% on testv4) and halves wrong locks on IMG_7888. On the USV, wrong leader boxes are rare (2.2% of candidates). Rejecting true boxes then causes drift, so the gate lowers accuracy. A loss-ratio sweep and target re-acquisition are planned.
  5. There is a trade-off between lag and smoothness. The learned trackers follow the target more closely but jitter more (USV clear: 1.6 → 1.9 px; drone testv4: 9 → 21–25 px).
Limitations of these results. There are only two drone videos, so the drone results come from two folds and are not significance-tested. Drone ground truth comes from Grounding DINO, not hand labels; frames where it was unsure (12% and 6%) are not scored. The USV results are simulation only and offline, with no closed-loop formation runs yet. Q was chosen from a grid.
